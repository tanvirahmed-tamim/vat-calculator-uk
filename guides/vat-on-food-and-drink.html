VAT on Food and Drink: Zero Rates, Takeaways and Drinks

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 26 November 2026 · 10 min read

Part of UK VAT rates

Food is not automatically VAT-free. Most groceries are zero-rated, but hot takeaway food, catering, confectionery, soft drinks and bottled water can move the same basket to 20%. The answer turns on the product, the temperature at the point of sale and how the customer consumes it.

In short

Most cold food bought for home consumption is zero-rated. Hot takeaway food, catering, confectionery, crisps, ice cream, soft drinks, bottled water sold as a beverage and alcohol are standard-rated at 20%. Cold takeaway food can stay zero-rated, but only when the product and the circumstances allow it.

Who this is for: Restaurants, cafes, takeaways, pubs, bakeries, food retailers, caterers and anyone selling food or drink.

General information: This guide provides general UK VAT information based on GOV.UK and HMRC guidance, including VAT Notice 701/14 and VAT Notice 709/1. It is not personal tax, legal or accounting advice. Check GOV.UK or ask a qualified UK accountant about your specific circumstances.

How Does UK VAT Apply to Food and Drink?

VAT on food and drink in the UK starts with a simple rule and then fills with exceptions. Most food of a kind used for human consumption is zero-rated when it is sold cold for consumption off the premises. Zero-rated is still a taxable supply: the seller records it on the VAT return and can normally recover input tax on related costs, but charges 0% output VAT.

The exceptions sit in HMRC VAT Notice 701/14 and Group 1 of Schedule 8 to the Value Added Tax Act 1994. Catering, hot takeaway food, confectionery, crisps, ice cream, soft drinks, bottled water sold as a beverage, fruit juice and alcohol are standard-rated at 20%. A food business therefore needs to look at the product and at the circumstances of sale, not at the word "food" on its own.

The £90,000 VAT registration threshold applies to taxable turnover, and zero-rated sales count towards it. A business that sells only zero-rated food can still have to register once its taxable turnover passes the threshold. Read the registration threshold guide for the test and the rolling 12 month calculation.

Do not treat general food or a takeaway as VAT-free across the board. A cold sandwich and a hot sausage roll can leave the same shop in the same bag and carry different rates. The rest of this guide separates the product rules from the hot food, drinks and takeaway rules.

Which Food and Drink Is Zero-Rated?

Most food intended for human consumption is zero-rated when it is sold cold for consumption off the premises. That includes bread, meat, fish, vegetables, fruit, dairy products, rice, pasta, flour and most ingredients used for home cooking.

Some drinks are zero-rated in their own right. Plain milk, flavoured milk drinks including milkshakes, tea, coffee, cocoa and drinking chocolate are zero-rated when sold as products for consumption off the premises. Plain soya or rice milk that is unflavoured and unsweetened is also zero-rated.

Zero-rated is not the same as exempt. The supply is taxable at 0%, it goes in the VAT return, and the business can normally recover input tax on costs linked to those sales. The zero-rated versus exempt guide explains why the distinction changes what a business can reclaim.

Which Food and Drink Is Standard-Rated?

A long list of food and drink is excluded from the zero rate and charged at 20%. The main categories are:

  • Confectionery, including chocolate, sweets and most cereal bars.
  • Crisps, roasted or salted nuts and similar savoury snacks.
  • Ice cream, ice lollies and similar frozen products.
  • Soft drinks, carbonated drinks, cordials, squashes and fruit juice.
  • Bottled, mineral, table and spa water held out for sale as a beverage.
  • Alcohol of all kinds.
  • Food sold for consumption on the premises, and hot takeaway food.

The product description matters more than the shop. A chocolate-covered biscuit is standard-rated, while a plain biscuit is normally zero-rated. A cake is zero-rated, even when it contains chocolate, unless it is a confectionery item by nature. A flapjack is zero-rated, while a sweet-tasting cereal bar is normally standard-rated.

The hospitality guide covers the service side: restaurants, cafes, pubs, catering and events. This page covers the product and takeaway liability.

How Do You Tell Hot Takeaway Food Apart from Cold Takeaway Food?

VAT Notice 709/1 sets a two stage test. First, the food or drink must be hot at the time it is provided to the customer. Hot means above the ambient air temperature. Second, at least one of five tests must apply. If the food is not hot at the point of sale, the hot food rules do not catch it, even if it was cooked earlier.

  1. The food was heated for the purpose of enabling it to be consumed hot.
  2. The food was heated to the customer's order.
  3. The food was kept hot after being heated.
  4. The food is provided in heat-retaining packaging, or in packaging specifically designed for hot food.
  5. The food is advertised or marketed in a way that indicates it is supplied hot.

The five tests catch more than an obvious hot counter. A pasty that is still warm because it came out of the oven a few minutes ago can stay zero-rated if the seller did not heat it to be eaten hot, did not heat it to order, did not keep it hot, used an ordinary paper bag and marketed it as "freshly baked" rather than "hot". Put the same pasty under a heat lamp, or sell it in a foil-lined bag, and the supply becomes standard-rated.

Heat-retaining packaging includes foil-lined bags, insulated containers and specially designed cardboard boxes. A pizza sold in a purpose-made box is the standard example. Ordinary paper bags and similar packaging do not trigger the test on their own.

If you provide a microwave for customers to heat food before or after the till, HMRC treats the supply as hot food and standard-rates it, whether or not you charge for the microwave. The same logic applies to a hot filling in cold bread: a bacon bap, hot dog, burger or kebab is standard-rated as a single item, even though part of it is cold.

The full five tests and the cooling examples are in VAT Notice 709/1. The hospitality guide applies them to eat-in service and catering.

Which Takeaway Foods, Baked Goods and Snacks Carry 20% VAT?

The following are normally standard-rated when sold hot or in a form caught by the hot food tests:

  • Fish and chips, chips and similar fried items.
  • Hot pizza, kebabs, burgers, hot dogs and hot sandwiches.
  • Indian, Chinese and other takeaway meals cooked to be eaten hot.
  • Pies, pasties, sausage rolls and similar savoury items kept hot or heated to order.
  • Baked potatoes with a hot or cold filling, and hot soup.
  • Toasted sandwiches, panini, garlic bread and teacakes heated to order.

The following are normally standard-rated whatever their temperature, because the product itself is excluded from the zero rate:

  • Confectionery, chocolate, sweets and sweet-tasting cereal bars.
  • Potato crisps and similar packaged savoury snacks.
  • Ice cream, sorbet, ice lollies and similar frozen products.
  • Soft drinks, bottled water, fruit juice and alcohol.
  • Chocolate-covered biscuits and other chocolate-coated bakery items.

Cakes are the exception that causes most confusion. A plain cake, sponge, fruit cake or flapjack is zero-rated. A cake supplied in the course of catering, or a cake that is hot and caught by the hot food tests, is standard-rated. A chocolate-covered biscuit is not a cake for this purpose and is standard-rated.

National chains such as Greggs, McDonald's and KFC apply the same legal tests as an independent takeaway or a local Indian restaurant. The brand does not set the rate. A cold sandwich sold to take away is usually zero-rated, while hot food, hot drinks and food kept hot or sold in heat-retaining packaging is standard-rated at 20%.

How Does VAT Work on Soft Drinks, Coffee, Tea and Alcohol?

VAT on soft drinks is standard-rated at 20%. That includes carbonated drinks, cola, lemonade, mixers, tonic, soda, cordials, squashes, fruit juice and juice concentrates. Bottled, mineral, table and spa water held out for sale as a beverage is also standard-rated. The idea that bottled water is always zero-rated is wrong.

Some drinks stay zero-rated in their own right. Plain milk, flavoured milk drinks including milkshakes, tea, coffee, cocoa and drinking chocolate are zero-rated when sold as products for consumption off the premises. Plain, unflavoured and unsweetened soya or rice milk is also zero-rated.

Temperature and service change the answer. All hot drinks are standard-rated, including tea, coffee and hot chocolate. Any drink sold for consumption on the premises is standard-rated, even if the drink itself would be zero-rated. A cold drink that is zero-rated in its own right, such as milk, can be treated as zero-rated when it is sold for consumption off the premises.

Alcohol is always standard-rated at 20%, whether it is sold in a shop, a pub or with a meal. Alcohol-free beer and wine are also standard-rated. The VAT rates guide covers the wider rate map.

Catering and Takeaway Food

Catering is standard-rated at 20%, whether it is a restaurant meal, a wedding buffet or food supplied at an event. Hot takeaway food is also standard-rated: if the food is heated, kept hot or sold hot at the point of sale, the zero rate does not apply.

Cold takeaway food is usually zero-rated. That is why a sandwich shop and a hot-food counter can sit next to each other and charge different rates. If you sell both, keep the product ranges and till records separate.

Delivery needs care. Under VAT Notice 700/24, a delivery charge that is part of a single supply of the goods follows the VAT treatment of those goods. A hot pizza and its delivery are standard-rated; a cold sandwich and its delivery are zero-rated. But a separate delivery service, or delivery of someone else's goods, is standard-rated at 20% even when the goods are zero-rated. VAT Notice 709/1 also treats delivery of cooked ready-to-eat meals as catering in some cases, so check the contract before you zero-rate the charge.

A £28.50 Mixed Takeaway Order

A customer buys a hot takeaway order for £24.00 and a cold sandwich for £4.50. The hot food is standard-rated, so the £24.00 gross contains £4.00 of VAT and £20.00 of net sales. The cold sandwich is zero-rated, so it contains no VAT. The order totals £28.50 gross, £24.50 net and £4.00 VAT.

To check the VAT on the hot part, multiply the gross by the VAT fraction: £24.00 × 20/120 = £4.00. The VAT calculation guide shows the same split for a whole takeaway order, and the VAT fraction calculator does the arithmetic.

Mixed Baskets and Meal Deals

A basket containing zero-rated and standard-rated items is not automatically taxed at one rate. Each item follows its own treatment. If you sell a hamper or a meal deal for a single price, apportion the price between the components on a fair and reasonable basis and show the VAT on the standard-rated part.

A composite supply, where the items are inseparable, can be treated as a single supply at the rate of the dominant element. A meal with a drink is usually a mixed supply, not a composite one, because the drink is separately identifiable. The hospitality guide works through a meal deal example.

What Records Should a Food Business Keep?

Food businesses need product-level records: what was sold, whether it was hot or cold, whether it was eaten on the premises, and how the price was split. Keep the menus, recipes, packaging specifications and till reports that support the treatment.

If you sell cold food from a site with seating, record how you apportion sales between on-premises and takeaway consumption. HMRC expects a fair and reasonable method supported by evidence, not a single rate applied to the whole till. The VAT record keeping guide covers the wider retention rules.

Common mistakes

  • Treating all takeaway food as zero-rated when hot food is standard-rated.
  • Zero-rating confectionery, crisps, soft drinks or bottled water.
  • Assuming a warm pasty is always standard-rated, or always zero-rated, without checking the five tests.
  • Missing the heat-retaining packaging test on foil-lined bags and pizza boxes.
  • Applying one rate to a mixed basket without apportioning.
  • Forgetting that a separate delivery service is standard-rated even when the food is zero-rated.
  • Zero-rating a hot drink because the tea or coffee product itself is zero-rated.

Questions

Is all food zero-rated for VAT?
No. Most cold food for home consumption is zero-rated, but catering, hot takeaway, confectionery, crisps, ice cream, soft drinks and alcohol are standard-rated at 20%.
Is takeaway food zero-rated?
Only cold takeaway food, and not all of it. Hot takeaway food is standard-rated at 20%, and so are confectionery, crisps and soft drinks.
Is a chocolate-covered biscuit zero-rated?
No, it is normally standard-rated as confectionery. A plain biscuit is zero-rated, and a cake is generally zero-rated even if it contains chocolate.
Do I charge VAT on delivery?
The delivery charge follows the contract. If delivery is part of a single supply of the food, the charge follows the food's rate. A separate delivery service is standard-rated at 20%.
How do I tax a meal deal?
Apportion the price between the zero-rated and standard-rated items on a fair and reasonable basis, and charge VAT on the standard-rated part.
Is alcohol ever zero-rated?
No. Alcohol is standard-rated at 20%, even when it is sold with food or as part of a meal.
Is there VAT on takeaway coffee?
Yes, if the coffee is sold hot. A hot drink is standard-rated at 20%, whether the customer drinks it in or takes it away. A cold drink that is zero-rated in its own right can be zero-rated when sold for off-premises consumption.
Is bottled water zero-rated?
No. Bottled, mineral, table and spa water held out for sale as a beverage is standard-rated at 20%. Plain tap water is not a bottled beverage supply.
Is there VAT on a Greggs or McDonald's takeaway?
The brand does not set the rate. Cold items sold to take away are usually zero-rated, while hot food, hot drinks and items kept hot or sold in heat-retaining packaging are standard-rated at 20%.
