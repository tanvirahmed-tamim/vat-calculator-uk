VAT Flat Rate Scheme vs Standard VAT: Which Is Better for Your UK Business?

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 25 November 2026 · 9 min read

Part of Registration, returns and compliance

Choosing between the UK VAT Flat Rate Scheme and standard VAT accounting comes down to how much VAT you incur on business purchases and whether the business triggers the 16.5% limited cost rule. Under standard VAT you pay HMRC the difference between the VAT you charge customers and the VAT you reclaim on eligible costs. Under the Flat Rate Scheme you pay a fixed percentage of VAT-inclusive turnover and usually cannot reclaim VAT on everyday purchases.

In short

Standard VAT usually wins where input VAT is high or turnover includes zero-rated or exempt income. The Flat Rate Scheme can win where relevant goods spending clears the 2% test and the sector rate is low. Most service businesses without significant goods purchases end up on the 16.5% limited cost rate.

Who this is for: UK sole traders, freelancers, contractors and limited companies comparing VAT schemes, especially businesses near the £150,000 joining limit.

General information: This guide is based on GOV.UK and HMRC VAT Notice 733. It is not personal tax or accounting advice. Check your sector rate and eligibility on GOV.UK, or confirm with a qualified UK accountant, before joining or leaving a VAT scheme.

How do the Flat Rate Scheme and standard VAT work?

Under standard VAT accounting you charge the appropriate rate on each taxable supply, reclaim input VAT on qualifying business costs that carry a valid VAT invoice, and pay HMRC the difference. If input VAT is higher than output VAT in a period, you claim the repayment instead.

The Flat Rate Scheme works differently. You still charge customers the normal rate and still issue VAT invoices, so your prices do not change. Instead of offsetting input VAT purchase by purchase, you multiply VAT-inclusive turnover by a flat rate set for your business sector and pay that amount to HMRC.

The flat rate is not a rate you charge customers. It is the percentage you use to work out what you pay HMRC. A 14.5% sector rate does not mean you add 14.5% to an invoice: you still add 20% to a standard-rated sale and account for the flat rate on the gross total. The VAT calculation guide covers the net-to-gross formulas behind both methods.

What is the 16.5% limited cost business rule?

Since 1 April 2017, a limited cost business pays 16.5% regardless of its sector. The rule stops service businesses with very little physical stock from using a low sector percentage to keep a large share of the output VAT they collect.

You are a limited cost business if your spending on relevant goods is either less than 2% of your VAT flat rate turnover, or more than 2% but less than £1,000 a year. For a quarterly return the £1,000 figure is pro-rated to £250. VAT flat rate turnover is VAT-inclusive and includes standard-rated, reduced-rate, zero-rated and exempt income.

What counts as relevant goods

Physical goods used exclusively for the business, such as stationery, printer consumables, cleaning products, raw materials, and stock bought for resale where selling goods is the main business activity. Standard software supplied on a disk can count; downloaded software is a service.

Services do not count. That includes cloud hosting, downloaded software, accountancy fees, insurance, commercial rent and subcontractors. Food and drink for you or your staff, vehicles, vehicle parts and fuel are also excluded, unless you run a transport business using your own or leased vehicles. Capital expenditure goods of any value are excluded from the test.

Because software, contractors and professional services sit outside the test, many freelancers, IT contractors, consultants and digital agencies find themselves on 16.5% if they join the Flat Rate Scheme. The test is applied to each VAT return period, so a business close to the 2% line can move between its sector rate and 16.5% from one quarter to the next.

In your first year of VAT registration the 1% discount reduces the limited cost rate to 15.5%. The discount runs for 12 months from the date you registered for VAT, not from the date you joined the scheme.

Worked example: Flat Rate Scheme vs standard VAT in GBP (£)

The figures below use a UK computer and IT consultancy with net sales of £80,000, all standard-rated. Output VAT at 20% is £16,000, so VAT-inclusive turnover is £96,000. VAT-inclusive expenses are £12,000, which includes £2,000 of input VAT.

Under standard VAT accounting the bill is output VAT of £16,000 minus input VAT of £2,000, which leaves £14,000.00 payable to HMRC.

Scenario A: relevant goods above the 2% test

Assume the consultancy spends £2,400 including VAT on office supplies, printer consumables and boxed software licences used exclusively in the business. That is 2.5% of £96,000, above the £1,920 threshold and above £1,000, so the limited cost rule does not apply. Computer and IT consultancy has a 14.5% sector rate.

  • 14.5% of £96,000 is £13,920.00, which is £80.00 less than standard VAT.
  • In the first year, 13.5% of £96,000 is £12,960.00, which is £1,040.00 less than standard VAT.

Scenario B: relevant goods below the 2% test

If the same consultancy spent only £600 including VAT on relevant goods, the 2% test is failed because £600 is below £1,920. The limited cost rate of 16.5% then applies.

  • 16.5% of £96,000 is £15,840.00, which is £1,840.00 more than standard VAT.
  • In the first year, 15.5% of £96,000 is £14,880.00, which is still £880.00 more than standard VAT.

A fully standard-rated business paying 16.5% of gross turnover pays the equivalent of 19.8% of net sales. That leaves almost no margin below the 20% standard rate, while input VAT on expenses stays blocked.

When does standard VAT beat the Flat Rate Scheme?

Standard VAT usually leaves you better off in these situations:

  • You trigger the 16.5% limited cost rate. 16.5% of gross turnover equals 19.8% of net sales for a fully standard-rated business, while input VAT on your costs remains blocked.
  • You carry significant VAT-bearing overheads or stock. Retailers, manufacturers, hospitality venues and growing businesses that buy equipment, stock or paid marketing from VAT-registered suppliers usually reclaim more input VAT under standard VAT.
  • You sell zero-rated or reduced-rate supplies. Flat rate turnover includes zero-rated and exempt income, so the flat rate can create a VAT cost on sales where you charged 0% or 5%.
  • Your turnover is above £150,000. You cannot join the scheme, and you must leave once VAT-inclusive income passes £230,000 unless HMRC accepts a forward look of £191,500 or less.
  • You regularly receive VAT repayments. The scheme is unsuitable if you usually reclaim more VAT than you charge, because the flat rate is designed for businesses that pay VAT over.

The reclaiming VAT guide explains which costs qualify for input tax recovery under standard VAT.

When can the Flat Rate Scheme still make sense?

The scheme can still be worthwhile in specific cases:

  • You clear the goods test and have a low sector rate. Trades, catering or retail businesses that buy enough qualifying physical goods to pass 2% and sit in a lower percentage band can pay less VAT than under standard VAT.
  • You are in your first year of VAT registration. The 1% discount lowers the flat rate for 12 months from registration, which improves cash retention if the 16.5% limited cost rate does not apply.
  • Your input VAT is minimal. If you buy few VAT-bearing goods or services, there is little input VAT to lose, and a single percentage of gross sales removes line-by-line expense tracking.
  • You want a predictable VAT bill. The flat rate moves with turnover rather than with the timing of purchase invoices, which can make budgeting simpler.

How do you join, switch or leave the Flat Rate Scheme?

How to join

Apply when you register for VAT, or later by submitting form VAT600FRS to HMRC. You must expect VAT-taxable turnover of £150,000 or less, excluding VAT, in the next 12 months. VAT-taxable turnover includes standard-rated, reduced-rate and zero-rated supplies but leaves out exempt supplies. HMRC confirms your start date, and you should not account on the flat rate basis before it. The registration threshold guide explains how the £150,000 test differs from the £90,000 registration threshold.

You cannot use the scheme with the Cash Accounting Scheme or a VAT margin scheme, and you cannot use it for supplies that fall under a domestic reverse charge. You can use it alongside Annual Accounting. If you are already in a VAT group, or have been eligible to join one in the last 24 months, check the eligibility rules before applying.

When you must leave

Check your turnover on each anniversary of joining and whenever income rises sharply. You must leave if your VAT-inclusive income in the year ending on that date is more than £230,000, excluding sales of capital assets, or if you expect income in the next 30 days alone to be more than £230,000. HMRC may allow you to stay if it is satisfied that your income in the next 12 months will not exceed £191,500.

How to leave voluntarily

Contact HMRC with your name, signature, VAT number, business name and address. HMRC confirms your leaving date. If you leave part-way through a VAT period, you do two calculations on the next return: the flat rate for the part of the period before you left, and normal VAT rules for the rest. You cannot rejoin the Flat Rate Scheme for 12 months after you leave.

Capital assets and switching to standard VAT

A single purchase of capital expenditure goods costing £2,000 or more including VAT can be claimed outside the scheme, on the normal VAT return. One invoice counts as one purchase. Several smaller purchases do not qualify just because their total passes £2,000. Leaving the scheme is the switch to standard VAT; there is no separate application, but you should keep records of stock and assets because adjustments can be needed.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a low sector rate applies without checking the 16.5% limited cost test.
  • Leaving zero-rated or exempt income out of flat rate turnover when working out the flat rate bill.
  • Trying to reclaim VAT on services, or on a laptop that cost less than £2,000.
  • Missing the first-year discount window, which runs from VAT registration rather than from joining the scheme.
  • Forgetting the 12-month rejoin lock-out after leaving the scheme.

Questions

Do I still charge 20% VAT on invoices under the Flat Rate Scheme?
Yes. You still charge customers the normal VAT rate, usually 20%, and show VAT on VAT invoices. The flat rate only changes how much of your VAT-inclusive turnover you pay to HMRC.
Can I reclaim VAT on a laptop or equipment under the Flat Rate Scheme?
You cannot reclaim VAT on everyday purchases or services, including a laptop costing under £2,000. The exception is a single purchase of capital expenditure goods costing £2,000 or more including VAT, such as a £2,400 computer bought on one invoice.
Does the 1% first-year discount apply to the 16.5% limited cost rate?
Yes. In your first year of VAT registration, the 1% discount reduces the 16.5% limited cost rate to 15.5% until the day before your first VAT registration anniversary.
Can I switch from the Flat Rate Scheme back to Standard VAT if my costs rise?
Yes. You can leave at any time by contacting HMRC, which will confirm your leaving date. You cannot rejoin the Flat Rate Scheme for 12 months after you leave.
