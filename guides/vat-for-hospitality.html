VAT for Hospitality: Restaurants, Pubs, Catering and Events

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 26 November 2026 · 10 min read

Part of VAT by business type

Hospitality sits on a set of fine lines: hot food versus cold, restaurant versus takeaway, room versus holiday let, and ordinary booking versus a tour operator package. The common thread is that eat-in service, hot food and accommodation are standard-rated at 20%, while most cold takeaway food stays zero-rated.

In short

Restaurant food, hot takeaways, catering and hotel accommodation are standard-rated at 20%. Most cold takeaway food is zero-rated. Tour operators can use the Tour Operators Margin Scheme, compulsory service charges are taxable, and delivery platforms split the food, commission and delivery into separate supplies.

Who this is for: Restaurants, cafes, pubs, takeaways, caterers, event organisers, hotels, guest houses, holiday lets and tour operators.

General information: This guide provides general UK VAT information based on GOV.UK and HMRC guidance, including VAT Notice 709/1, VAT Notice 709/3 and VAT Notice 700/65. It is not personal tax, legal or accounting advice. Check GOV.UK or ask a qualified UK accountant about your specific circumstances.

Food and drink

Food sold for consumption on the premises, such as a restaurant meal, is standard-rated at 20%. The same applies to catering supplied at an event. Most food sold cold for consumption off the premises is zero-rated, but confectionery, crisps, soft drinks and similar items are standard-rated.

Alcohol is always standard-rated. If a meal includes a drink, the VAT treatment of each element follows its own rules; a mixed supply can be treated as a single supply when the elements are inseparable.

Do not treat hospitality food as VAT-free across the board. The food and drink guide owns the product-level rules, including the five hot food tests in VAT Notice 709/1. This page applies those rules to the venue, the event and the bill.

Restaurants, Cafes and Pubs

A restaurant, cafe or pub charges VAT at 20% on food and drink that the customer consumes on the premises. That covers a sit-down meal, a bar snack, a coffee drunk at a table and a pint served in the garden. The rate applies to the service of food, not just to the product, so a zero-rated cold sandwich becomes standard-rated when it is eaten in.

The same outlet can sell at two rates. A pub that sells a cold sandwich to take away can zero-rate it, while the same sandwich eaten at a table is standard-rated. A cafe with a takeaway counter has the same split. Keep the two streams separate at the till and in your records, because HMRC will look for evidence that the split is real.

Alcohol is always standard-rated, whether it is sold in a restaurant, a pub or a shop. Soft drinks, bottled water sold as a beverage and fruit juice are standard-rated too. A pub meal with a pint is therefore 20% throughout, with no zero-rated element.

The boundary matters most for small outlets. If you sell both hot and cold food, keep the product ranges and the till records separate so the return can be supported. A single mixed counter does not make everything standard-rated, but poor records make the zero-rated share hard to defend.

Hot food that is not obviously hot

Food is standard-rated if it is heated to above ambient temperature for the purpose of being sold hot, or if it is kept hot. Food that has been cooked and then cooled for sale cold is not automatically caught. The test is the state of the food when it is sold, not how it was prepared.

VAT Notice 709/1 adds four more tests. Food is caught when it is heated to the customer's order, kept hot after being heated, supplied in heat-retaining packaging, or advertised as hot. A pasty that is merely warm can stay zero-rated if none of the tests applies. Put it under a heat lamp, heat it to order or sell it in a foil-lined bag and it becomes standard-rated.

Catering and Events

Catering is standard-rated at 20%, whether it is a wedding buffet, a corporate lunch, a festival food stall or a private dinner. The rate follows the service of providing food and drink ready to eat, so it does not matter whether the customer collects the food, has it delivered or eats it at the venue.

Event admission is standard-rated at 20%. If a ticket includes food, drink or a programme, the price is normally a single standard-rated supply. Some charity fundraising events can be exempt when the conditions are met, so check the charity VAT guide before you assume the event is taxable.

A caterer who supplies staff, equipment and food as one package is providing a single service. The whole charge is standard-rated, even if some of the food would be zero-rated if sold cold in a shop. Keep the contract and the event paperwork so the treatment is clear.

A worked bill

A restaurant bill is £200 for food and drink, plus a compulsory 12.5% service charge of £25. The total is £225 net, and VAT at 20% is £45, giving £270 gross.

If the service charge were genuinely discretionary and paid straight to staff, it would generally be outside the scope of VAT. The more common arrangement, where the charge is added automatically and the business handles the money, is taxable.

A worked meal deal

A cafe sells a meal deal for £6.00: a cold sandwich, a bag of crisps and a bottled drink. The sandwich is zero-rated, while the crisps and the drink are standard-rated. The business apportions the £6.00 on a fair and reasonable basis, for example £3.00 for the sandwich, £1.50 for the crisps and £1.50 for the drink.

Accommodation

Hotel accommodation, guest house rooms, bed and breakfast and holiday lets are standard-rated at 20% when you are VAT-registered. The temporary 5% rate ended on 30 September 2021; a 12.5% interim rate ran until 31 March 2022, and the standard rate returned on 1 April 2022.

A booking deposit is part of the accommodation supply and follows the same rate. If you also supply meals or parking, treat those elements according to their own rules.

Cancellation charges are not automatically outside the scope. HMRC's VAT Notice 709/3 treats a deposit as an advance payment, so VAT is due when it is received. If the guest cancels or does not arrive and you keep the money, the VAT stays due; it can only be reduced to the extent that you refund the customer. The same logic applies to a card guarantee that is charged after a no-show.

The furnished holiday lettings income tax regime was abolished from April 2025, but that did not change the VAT rate on holiday accommodation, which remains 20% once registered.

Tour operators

The Tour Operators Margin Scheme (TOMS) lets a tour operator account for VAT on the margin on certain travel packages rather than on the full selling price. It applies to travel, accommodation and other services bought in and resold as part of a package.

Under TOMS the invoice normally does not show VAT separately. The margin is calculated for the accounting period, and there are special rules for business customers and for sales outside the UK.

TOMS worked example

A package sells for £1,200 and the supplier costs are £900. The margin is £300. VAT at 20% on the margin is £50, found by multiplying £300 by 1/6. The customer pays £1,200, and the VAT is accounted for on the return rather than added to the invoice.

Service Charges, Tips and Delivery Platforms

A compulsory service charge is taxable at the same rate as the principal supply, which for hospitality is 20%. A genuinely discretionary tip, where the customer is free to decide and the staff receive it, is outside the scope of VAT. Keep the treatment consistent and document it.

The distinction is about control, not wording. A line labelled "optional" that is added by default and retained by the business is still part of the price.

Delivery platforms such as Deliveroo, Just Eat and Uber Eats add another layer. The restaurant supplies the food, the platform supplies a marketplace and delivery service, and the customer usually pays the platform. The restaurant accounts for VAT on the food it supplies, and the platform accounts for VAT on its commission and delivery fees. Where the platform acts as principal, it may account for VAT on the full charge instead.

Check the contract and the invoices. Do not assume that the platform's VAT treatment matches yours, and do not net the commission off the food sale without a clear record of the two supplies.

What has changed

  • 2021-22: the temporary 5% rate for hospitality and tourism ended on 30 September 2021, a 12.5% interim rate ran to 31 March 2022, and the standard 20% rate returned on 1 April 2022.
  • 2025: the furnished holiday lettings income tax and corporation tax regime was abolished from April 2025; the VAT treatment of holiday accommodation did not change.
  • 2026: the temporary zero rate for domestic electricity in Great Britain affects utility bills, not the VAT rate on food, rooms or events.

Common mistakes

  • Treating all takeaway food as zero-rated when hot food is standard-rated.
  • Zero-rating bottled water sold as a beverage, which is standard-rated at 20%.
  • Applying the old 5% hospitality rate after 30 September 2021.
  • Showing VAT separately on a TOMS invoice.
  • Treating a compulsory service charge as a discretionary tip.
  • Assuming the platform's VAT treatment matches the restaurant's.
  • Assuming the 2025 furnished holiday lettings change cut VAT on holiday lets.

Questions

Is takeaway food zero-rated?
Only cold takeaway food, and not all of it. Hot takeaway food is standard-rated at 20%, and confectionery, crisps and soft drinks are also standard-rated.
Is VAT charged on hotel rooms?
Yes, at 20% when the hotel is VAT-registered. The temporary 5% rate ended on 30 September 2021, a 12.5% interim rate ran to 31 March 2022, and the standard rate returned on 1 April 2022.
What is TOMS?
The Tour Operators Margin Scheme. It lets a tour operator account for VAT on the margin on certain travel packages instead of the full selling price.
Do I charge VAT on tips?
A compulsory service charge is taxable at the same rate as the principal supply, usually 20%. A genuinely discretionary tip paid to staff is outside the scope of VAT.
Do I charge VAT on event tickets?
Yes, admission is standard-rated at 20%. Some charity fundraising events can be exempt when the conditions are met.
Do restaurants and pubs always charge 20% VAT?
Food and drink served for consumption on the premises is standard-rated at 20%, so a restaurant, cafe or pub meal is normally 20%. A pub that sells cold takeaway food can still zero-rate that part, but the eat-in and hot food sales stay at 20%.
How do I charge VAT on a meal deal?
Apportion the single price between the items on a fair and reasonable basis, then charge VAT on the standard-rated items. A sandwich, a bag of crisps and a bottled drink in one deal usually splits into zero-rated food and standard-rated snacks and drink.
How does VAT work with delivery platforms?
The platform usually charges the customer and passes the food payment to the restaurant, so the restaurant accounts for VAT on the food it supplies. Commission and delivery fees are separate supplies, and a platform that acts as principal may account for VAT on the full charge. Check the contract.
