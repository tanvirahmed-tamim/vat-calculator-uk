How to Add VAT to a Price: 20%, 5% and Custom Rates

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 19 November 2026 · 5 min read

Part of VAT calculation

Adding VAT is a single multiplication, but small rounding choices can change the figure on an invoice. This guide sets out the VAT formula, a net to gross example and the 20% VAT method, with worked cases.

In short

Multiply the net amount by 1 plus the rate as a decimal. At 20%, multiply by 1.20; at 5%, multiply by 1.05. Round the VAT to the nearest penny, then add it to the net. Different rounding rules apply to retailers and to businesses issuing VAT invoices.

The formula

To add VAT to a net amount:

Gross = Net × (1 + rate ÷ 100)

The bracket converts the percentage into a multiplier. At 20% it is 1.20; at 5% it is 1.05; at 0% it is 1.00.

The VAT element is simply the gross minus the net, or equivalently Net × rate ÷ 100.

Worked examples

Standard rate at 20%

A consultancy invoice has a net value of £250. Multiply by 1.20 to get £300. The VAT is £50 and the gross is £300.

Reduced rate at 5% on domestic gas

A domestic gas bill has a net value of £80. Multiply by 1.05 to get £84. The VAT is £4.

Domestic electricity in Great Britain is different at the moment: qualifying supplies are temporarily zero-rated from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027, so the multiplier is 1.00. Northern Ireland electricity stays at 5%, and gas and other domestic fuels stay at 5% across the UK. Read VAT on energy for the detail.

A price with pence

A net price of £49.99 at 20% produces VAT of £9.998 before rounding. Normal rounding to the nearest penny gives £10.00 and a gross of £59.99. An invoice trader using the Notice 700 paragraph 17.5 concession may instead round the VAT down to £9.99, making the gross £59.98.

Zero rate

Zero-rated supplies, such as most plain food, have a multiplier of 1.00. The gross equals the net and the VAT is £0.00, but the supply is still taxable and belongs on the VAT return.

Rounding

HMRC applies two different rules, and mixing them up is the usual cause of a penny dispute with a customer.

  • Retailers calculating VAT at line or invoice level must not round the VAT figure down. They may round each individual calculation up or down to the nearest penny (VAT Notice 700, paragraph 17.6).
  • Businesses issuing VAT invoices to other businesses may use the concession in VAT Notice 700, paragraph 17.5: round the total VAT payable on the invoice down to a whole penny and ignore any fraction. The concession is designed for invoice traders and only applies where the VAT charged to the customer is the same VAT paid to HMRC.

Pick one method for an invoice and apply it to every line. Keep a note of the method used so a later check can reproduce the figures.

Why per-line and per-total rounding differ

Take five lines at £1.12 net each, all at 20%:

Five lines at £1.12 net, 20% VAT
MethodCalculationVAT
Per line£0.224 rounded to £0.22 × 5£1.10
On the total£5.60 net × 20%£1.12

The 2p gap is not an error in either method. It is the reason not to switch methods part-way through an invoice.

On an invoice

A VAT invoice needs to show the rate and the VAT amount clearly. A simple layout:

DescriptionNetVAT rateVATGross

If you use accounting software, it will normally apply the rounding for you. Check the first few invoices against a manual calculation before you rely on it.

Common mistakes

  • Adding 20p per pound instead of 20%. VAT is a percentage of the net, not a fixed cash amount.
  • Applying the rate to the gross. Adding VAT starts from the net figure.
  • Assuming every sale is 20%. Domestic gas and other domestic fuel is 5%, most food is 0%, and some residential renovations and conversions are 5%.
  • Treating zero-rated as exempt. Zero-rated supplies still go on the return and usually allow input VAT recovery.
  • Mixing rates on one line. Group lines by rate before calculating the VAT.

Questions

Do I add VAT before or after a discount?
Apply the discount to the net price first, then add VAT to the discounted amount. VAT is charged on the price the customer actually pays, so a 10% discount on a £100 net price leaves £90 net and £108 gross at 20%.
Should I round VAT per line or on the invoice total?
Pick one method per invoice and apply it consistently. Retailers must not round the VAT figure down, but may round each calculation up or down to the nearest penny. Businesses issuing VAT invoices to other businesses may use the Notice 700 paragraph 17.5 concession and round the total VAT down to a whole penny.
Which rate do I add?
Use the rate that applies to the supply at the tax point. Most goods and services are standard rated at 20%, some are reduced at 5%, and zero-rated supplies use a multiplier of 1.00.
