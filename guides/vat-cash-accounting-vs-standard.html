VAT Cash Accounting Scheme vs. Standard Accounting: When It Helps Cash Flow

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 25 November 2026 · 9 min read

Part of Registration, returns and compliance

Cash accounting and standard accounting both produce the same VAT over time, but they move the tax point. This guide compares the two methods, the £1.35 million entry test and the cases where standard accounting is the better choice.

In short

Cash accounting moves the VAT tax point to the date money moves: you pay output VAT when customers pay you and reclaim input VAT when you pay suppliers. You can join with estimated taxable turnover of £1.35 million or less and must leave above £1.6 million. Standard accounting usually suits businesses that pay suppliers quickly and want input VAT back sooner.

Who this is for: UK VAT-registered owners, bookkeepers and finance managers deciding how to account for VAT, and anyone whose customers pay on long credit terms.

What is the VAT Cash Accounting Scheme?

Cash accounting is a VAT scheme where you account for VAT on your sales when your customer pays you, and reclaim VAT on your purchases when you pay your supplier. The tax point moves from the invoice date to the date money moves.

Most businesses use standard accounting, where VAT is due on the return that covers the invoice or supply date, whether or not the customer has paid. That is why an unpaid invoice can still cost you a fifth of its value in cash.

Cash accounting reverses that. A £10,000 invoice at 20% carries £2,000 of output tax, and under cash accounting that £2,000 only becomes payable when the £12,000 reaches your bank account. If the customer never pays, you never owe the VAT on that sale.

The scheme is set out in HMRC's VAT Notice 731 and summarised on the GOV.UK Cash Accounting Scheme page.

Cash accounting vs standard VAT accounting

Standard accounting uses the invoice or supply date as the tax point. Cash accounting uses the date you receive or make payment. Every other difference follows from that single change.

Cash accounting and standard accounting side by side
Feature Cash accounting Standard accounting
Tax point on sales Date payment is received Invoice date or supply date
Tax point on purchases Date you pay the supplier Invoice date or supply date
Output VAT due When the customer pays When you invoice or supply
Input VAT reclaimed When you pay the supplier When you hold a valid VAT invoice
Bad debt relief Not needed on sales, the VAT was never paid Claim after 6 months unpaid (VAT Notice 700/18)
Turnover test to join £1.35 million estimated taxable turnover No scheme limit
Record keeping Payments in and out, matched to invoices Sales and purchase invoices

Who can use the Cash Accounting Scheme?

You can join if you are VAT registered and your estimated VAT taxable turnover for the next 12 months is £1.35 million or less. HMRC uses estimated turnover, so a business expecting a quieter year can qualify even after a stronger one.

You must leave the scheme once taxable turnover passes £1.6 million. It is not available at all if you use the Flat Rate Scheme, are behind with VAT returns or payments, or have committed a VAT offence in the last 12 months.

Some transactions must stay on standard accounting even while the rest of the business uses cash accounting:

  • invoices with payment terms of 6 months or more;
  • invoices raised in advance of the supply;
  • goods bought or sold under lease purchase, hire purchase, conditional sale or credit sale;
  • imports of goods into Northern Ireland from the EU;
  • removals of goods from a customs warehouse.

Reverse charge supplies also stay on standard accounting, because the customer must report the output tax and input tax on the same return. The VAT reverse charge UK guide explains the construction and specified goods rules.

The turnover figure is taxable turnover, which means sales that are not exempt. Zero-rated sales still count. Check the registration threshold calculator if you are near a boundary, and the registration and returns guide for how the rolling 12-month test works.

When cash accounting helps cash flow, and when standard is better

Cash accounting helps when customers pay later than you pay suppliers, or when customers are simply slow. A service business that invoices in month one and is paid in month three keeps the VAT for two extra months.

Take a £12,000 invoice including VAT: £10,000 net plus £2,000 VAT. Under standard accounting the £2,000 is due on the return covering the invoice, even if the bank balance has not moved. Under cash accounting it is due on the return covering the payment. On 90-day terms that is roughly one VAT quarter of extra room.

Standard accounting is often better in the opposite case. If you pay suppliers quickly but customers pay on time, or if you hold stock and want input VAT back before you sell it, standard accounting returns the cash sooner. Retailers and businesses with high stock levels usually find standard accounting gives the stronger cash position.

Cash accounting also removes most bad debt VAT risk. If a customer never pays, you never account for the output tax, so there is no bad debt relief claim to make and no six-month wait.

How input VAT works under the scheme

Input VAT is reclaimed when you pay your supplier, not when the invoice arrives. A £500 invoice plus £100 VAT gives you a £100 input tax claim in the period that includes the payment date.

That delay matters on long credit terms. Buying £20,000 of stock plus £4,000 VAT on 60-day terms means the £4,000 is not reclaimable until you settle the bill. Standard accounting would have returned it on the next return after the invoice.

Keep the VAT invoice regardless. HMRC can ask to see the invoice that supports the claim and the payment record that fixes the date. The rules on what a valid invoice must show are covered in VAT invoices and MTD.

Cash accounting and Making Tax Digital

Making Tax Digital does not change which scheme you use, but it does change what you have to keep. The payment date that fixes each tax point has to sit in your digital records, not only on a bank statement or in a separate payments file.

Most accounting software handles this when cash accounting is switched on in the VAT settings. The risk sits with spreadsheets and part-digital systems, where invoices are recorded in one place and payments in another. Every payment then needs a digital link back to the invoice it settles, because HMRC expects the audit trail to be visible without manual re-keying.

Part payments need the same care. If a customer pays half an invoice, only the VAT on the amount actually received becomes due. Software that marks the whole invoice as paid creates an output tax liability that has not happened yet, and it will not reverse itself when the rest of the money fails to arrive.

Check the timing of refunds too. A refund you issue reduces the consideration for the original sale, and the adjustment belongs in the period you repay the money, not the period of the original invoice.

Can you use cash accounting with the Flat Rate Scheme?

No. The Flat Rate Scheme and cash accounting cannot be combined. A business on the Flat Rate Scheme charges VAT at its flat rate percentage on gross turnover, and that scheme has its own rules on when the VAT is accounted for, so there is no room for a second tax point.

The choice between them usually comes down to where the value sits. A service business with few purchases often pays less under the Flat Rate Scheme. A business that buys a lot of standard-rated stock usually recovers more through normal input tax recovery, and cash accounting then adds a timing benefit on top.

Compare a full year rather than one quarter. Scheme changes happen at VAT period boundaries, and a switch that looks marginal in a quiet month can look very different once the seasonal peak is included.

Moving in and out of the scheme

You join at the start of a VAT accounting period and you do not have to tell HMRC. Leave at the end of a period, also without notifying HMRC, but you must report and pay any VAT still outstanding on sales you have made.

That catch-up payment can be spread over 6 months. If taxable turnover exceeded £1.35 million in the last 3 months, the outstanding VAT is payable straight away instead. Pay immediately as well if HMRC has written to withdraw your use of the scheme.

Switching from standard to cash accounting is usually simple for sales already invoiced and paid. Look closely at invoices issued and unpaid at the switch date, because those amounts may become payable only when the cash arrives. Switching out is the case that needs planning, since the catch-up lands on one return.

Use the VAT on expenses calculator to check reclaimable input tax on a gross receipt, and the bulk VAT calculator when you need to restate a run of invoices under a different method.

A quarter under both methods

Suppose a consultancy invoices £30,000 net in a quarter at 20% and is paid £20,000 of it by the quarter end. It also receives a £6,000 net supplier invoice on 30-day terms and pays it before the quarter end.

One quarter, standard accounting and cash accounting
Item Standard accounting Cash accounting
Output VAT £6,000 on all £30,000 invoiced £4,000 on £20,000 received
Input VAT £1,200 on the £6,000 invoice £1,200, supplier paid
VAT payable £4,800 £2,800
Difference £2,000 paid early £2,000 deferred to the next return

The £2,000 is deferred, not saved. When the customer pays in the next quarter, that return picks it up. The benefit is timing, and for a growing business timing can be the difference between needing an overdraft and not.

The same numbers look very different when nothing is collected. If the customer settles the whole £30,000 invoice 120 days later, standard accounting still puts £6,000 of output tax on the original return. Cash accounting leaves output tax at nil until the money arrives, which is exactly the protection a business chasing slow payers needs.

Should you join the scheme?

Join when the cash arrives after you have already paid the VAT on your costs, and leave it alone when the opposite is true. The table below sets out the patterns that usually decide it.

Which method usually suits which business
Pattern Better fit Why
Slow-paying customers, fast supplier payments Cash accounting Output tax waits for the cash, input tax is claimed on payment
Fast-paying customers, long supplier terms Standard accounting Input tax comes back before the supplier is paid
High stock levels and regular restocking Standard accounting Stock purchases generate input tax before the stock sells
Turnover close to £1.6 million Plan the exit Leaving triggers a catch-up payment on unpaid sales
Consumer deposits taken in advance Either, with care The deposit creates its own tax point when received

Turnover near the exit threshold deserves a plan rather than a surprise. If the business is growing, model the catch-up payment before it lands, and time any large invoices so the exit does not coincide with a quiet quarter.

Where cash accounting goes wrong

Most errors come from mixing the two methods in the records rather than from the scheme itself.

  • Invoices with 6-month payment terms sit outside the scheme and stay on the invoice date.
  • Deposits and advance payments create their own tax point when received, so cash accounting does not always defer the VAT.
  • Refunds, chargebacks and payment fees need matching to the original supply rather than to the bank line alone.
  • Partial exemption and capital goods scheme workings still rely on the underlying invoices, not just the statement.
  • Making Tax Digital records must capture the payment date that fixes each tax point as well as the invoice date.

If a return is already late, fixing the records is the priority. The penalties and inspections guide explains how late submission points and payment penalties accumulate.

Questions

What is the VAT Cash Accounting Scheme and how does it work?
Cash accounting is a scheme where you account for VAT on sales when your customer pays, and reclaim VAT on purchases when you pay your supplier. The tax point follows the money rather than the invoice date.
What is the difference between cash accounting and standard VAT accounting?
Standard accounting uses the invoice or supply date as the tax point, while cash accounting uses the date payment is made or received. The total VAT over time is the same, but the timing differs.
Who can use the VAT Cash Accounting Scheme in the UK?
VAT-registered businesses with estimated taxable turnover of £1.35 million or less in the next 12 months can join. You must leave once turnover passes £1.6 million, and the Flat Rate Scheme cannot be combined with it.
When does cash accounting help cash flow, and when is standard accounting better?
Cash accounting helps when customers pay later than you pay suppliers, because the output VAT follows the cash. Standard accounting is better when you pay suppliers quickly and want input VAT back sooner.
How is input VAT reclaimed under the Cash Accounting Scheme?
Input VAT is reclaimed in the VAT period that includes the date you pay your supplier, not the date on the invoice. Keep the VAT invoice and the payment record that fixes that date.
Can I switch from standard VAT accounting to the Cash Accounting Scheme?
Yes. You join at the start of a VAT accounting period if you meet the £1.35 million test, and you do not need to notify HMRC.
Do I need bad debt relief if I use cash accounting?
No. Output VAT is only accounted for when the customer pays, so an unpaid invoice never creates VAT to relieve.
