VAT Rounding and Mixed-Rate Invoices: How to Get It Right

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 19 November 2026 · 7 min read

Part of VAT calculation

Rounding is a small issue until a mixed-rate invoice has dozens of lines. The rule is to use normal rounding, be consistent, and make sure the VAT total reconciles to the invoice.

In short

Retailers must not round the VAT figure down, but may round each calculation up or down to the nearest penny. Businesses issuing VAT invoices to other businesses may use the Notice 700 paragraph 17.5 concession and round the total VAT down to a whole penny. Pick one method per invoice.

Who this is for: Anyone who issues VAT invoices, especially retailers, tradespeople and businesses selling at more than one rate.

Rounding

VAT is normally rounded to the nearest penny: half a penny or more rounds up, below half a penny rounds down. HMRC then applies two different rules depending on who is doing the rounding.

  • Retailers calculating VAT at line or invoice level must not round the VAT figure down. They may round each individual calculation up or down to the nearest penny (VAT Notice 700, paragraph 17.6).
  • Businesses issuing VAT invoices to other businesses may use the concession in VAT Notice 700, paragraph 17.5: round the total VAT payable on the invoice down to a whole penny and ignore any fraction. It only applies where the VAT charged to the customer is the VAT paid to HMRC.

Do not truncate the VAT on every line and call it rounding. Repeated truncation understates output tax and is difficult to defend in an inspection.

Per line or total

You can calculate VAT on each line and add the rounded amounts, or calculate VAT on the total for each rate. Both are used in practice, but they can produce different answers, so pick one method for an invoice and stay with it.

The method also needs to match the customer. A retailer cannot use the invoice-trader round-down, and a business-to-business invoice cannot switch between methods halfway through the page.

A worked comparison

Take five lines at £1.12 net each, all at 20%:

Five lines at £1.12 net, 20% VAT
MethodCalculationVAT
Per line£0.224 rounded to £0.22 × 5£1.10
On the total£5.60 net × 20%£1.12

The 2p gap is not an error in either method. It is the reason not to switch methods part-way through an invoice.

Now take a single line of £49.99 net at 20%. The VAT is £9.998. Normal rounding gives £10.00 and a gross of £59.99. An invoice trader using the paragraph 17.5 concession may instead round the VAT down to £9.99, making the gross £59.98. A retailer could not round £9.998 down.

Mixed-rate invoices

A mixed-rate invoice contains supplies at different rates: for example, standard-rated labour and zero-rated materials, or a meal with a soft drink. The invoice must show the net amount and VAT for each rate, not a single blended rate.

Apportion the price on a fair and reasonable basis. If the customer pays a single inclusive price, you still need to identify the consideration for each element. A blended rate that does not reflect the underlying supplies is not a valid substitute.

The food and drink guide has a worked example of a mixed basket, and the property guide covers the 0%, 5% and 20% split on building work.

Composite supplies

A composite supply is a single supply with several elements that are inseparable. It takes the rate of the dominant element. A multiple supply is several distinct supplies that can be priced separately, and each takes its own rate.

The distinction matters. A hotel room with breakfast included is usually a single supply of accommodation, while a meal and a bottle of wine are separate supplies with different treatments. Look at what the customer is buying, not just at the invoice lines.

Invoice presentation

A full VAT invoice must show the supplier's name, address and VAT number, the customer's details, the invoice date, the tax point if different, a description of the goods or services, the net amount, the rate and the VAT for each rate, and the total.

A simplified invoice can be used for retail supplies up to £250, with fewer details. Keep the invoice and a note of the rounding method with the VAT record for six years.

Common mistakes

  • Truncating VAT instead of rounding it.
  • Showing a single blended rate for a mixed-rate invoice.
  • Using different rounding methods on similar invoices.
  • Treating a multiple supply as composite to avoid showing two rates.

Related terms

Rounding, Mixed supply, Composite supply, VAT invoice.

Questions

Should I round VAT per line or on the invoice total?
Pick one method per invoice and apply it to every line. Per-line rounding and total rounding can differ by a penny or two; switching method part-way is what causes a reconciliation problem.
Does HMRC require a specific rounding method?
No single method is imposed, but the rules differ by trader. Retailers must not round the VAT figure down, though they may round each calculation up or down. Businesses issuing VAT invoices to other businesses may use the paragraph 17.5 concession to round the total VAT down to a whole penny.
How do I show VAT on a mixed-rate invoice?
Show the net amount and VAT for each rate separately, with a total for each. Do not use a single blended rate.
What is a composite supply?
A single supply with several inseparable elements, taxed at the rate of the dominant element. A multiple supply has distinct elements that are priced separately.
Can I use a simplified invoice?
Yes, for retail supplies up to £250. A simplified invoice needs fewer details than a full VAT invoice.
What if my invoice total differs by a penny from the VAT calculation?
A small rounding difference is normal. Choose a consistent method, make sure the invoice total and the VAT shown reconcile, and keep a note of the method used.
